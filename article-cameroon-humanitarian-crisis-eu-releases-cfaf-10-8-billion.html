OPINION: Cameroon Humanitarian Crisis — “EU Releases CFAF 10.8 Billion to Feed Cameroonians”

By Dr. David Makongo · May 7, 2026

OPINION

Surprisingly, reports suggest that the European Union, in particular, has released CFAF 10.8 billion to support humanitarian efforts to feed vulnerable Cameroonians, alongside other nations such as India and China providing assistance in cash and in kind. Many people ask an honest question: how can this even be possible in a country that is richly endowed with resources?

Let us approach this conversation with honesty, but also with respect—for ourselves and for Cameroon. Cameroon is not a poor country in resources; it is a country struggling with how those resources are managed, distributed, and protected.

When a nation so richly endowed still faces hunger, we are forced to ask difficult questions. Not to accuse in anger, but to understand in good faith. Right? Right.

1) Is this mainly a question of governance?

Is the challenge rooted in governance and mismanagement—leakage, weak oversight, unfair distribution, and the failure to translate wealth into measurable human security? If resources exist, then hunger must also raise questions about where priorities go, who is held accountable, and how public institutions perform.

2) Are we losing control of our economic direction?

Over time, we may have collectively lost (or failed to secure) control of our economic direction—waiting, perhaps too long, for solutions from outside while reforms remained incomplete at home. Aid can help in an emergency, but it cannot replace long-term planning, productive investment, and a national commitment to protect livelihoods and food systems.

3) Do external actors ever benefit from our divisions?

And it is also possible that, in some cases, humanitarian action exists alongside political and economic interests. History teaches that the so-called “international community” does not always act from goodwill alone. Sometimes, it responds to opportunities created by instability, weakness, and internal division. That possibility does not negate the value of aid; it simply demands that we examine motives, outcomes, and accountability.

These are not comfortable questions. But they are necessary ones.

Aid is not the problem. Dependence without reflection is.

Accepting humanitarian support is not a crime—many nations receive help in difficult moments. The problem begins when aid becomes a substitute for responsibility, when dependency is normalized, and when reforms are delayed because relief arrives from elsewhere.

A country blessed with oil, minerals, fertile land, and hardworking people should not quietly become the kind of nation that waits to be fed. We must ensure assistance reaches those who need it most, while strengthening systems that prevent the same crisis from repeating.

What responsibility looks like

Respectfully, the issue may not be a single cause. It may be a combination of internal challenges and external interests. If that is true, then the solution must begin with us: through accountability, unity, and a renewed commitment to take ownership of our economic future.

Let us prioritize transparency in how resources are managed. Let us protect vulnerable populations with better targeting, stronger coordination, and real monitoring. Let us defend local food production and livelihoods, so that hunger is not treated as “normal.” And let us rebuild unity—not as a slogan, but as a practical national agreement on fairness, service, and the rule of law.

Because no nation can outsource its dignity—or its destiny.

Thank you for reading and sharing.

Dr. David Makongo
